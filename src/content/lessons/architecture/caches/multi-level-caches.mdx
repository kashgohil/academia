One cache cannot be both fast and large. A larger cache has longer wires and more ways to compare, so it needs more cycles. So CPUs stack levels. A small L1 answers in about 4 cycles, a larger L2 in about 12, and a shared L3 in about 40. DRAM takes a few hundred cycles. Each level catches some of the misses of the level above it.

## Average memory access time

The **average memory access time** (AMAT) is the mean time of one access over all accesses. The demo below follows 1,000 accesses through the levels.

<AmatFunnelDemo />

1. Keep the defaults: miss rates of 5%, 40% and 30%. Of 1,000 accesses, 950 end in L1, and only 6 go to memory. The average is 6.6 cycles.
2. Read the bottom bar. The 6 accesses to memory add 1.2 cycles, which is 18% of the average time.
3. Set the L1 miss rate to 1% and the L2 local miss rate to 80%. L2 misses most of what reaches it, but the average is only 4.92 cycles.
4. Set the L1 miss rate to 20%, with the other rates at their defaults. The average goes up to 14.4 cycles.

<Steps>
<div>For one level: $\text{AMAT} = t_{\text{hit}} + m \cdot t_{\text{miss}}$, where $m$ is the miss rate.</div>
<div>The miss penalty of a level is the AMAT of the next level: $\text{AMAT} = t_1 + m_1\,(t_2 + m_2\,(t_3 + m_3\, t_{\text{mem}}))$.</div>
<div>Here $m_2$ is the <b>local</b> miss rate: the misses at L2 divided by the accesses that <i>reach</i> L2. The <b>global</b> miss rate is the misses divided by all accesses, $m_1 m_2$. A high local L2 miss rate can be acceptable when L1 already catches most accesses.</div>
<div>Example: $t = 4, 12, 40, 200$ and local miss rates of $5\%, 40\%, 30\%$ give $4 + 0.05(12 + 0.4(40 + 0.3 \cdot 200)) = 6.6$ cycles.</div>
</Steps>

## Inclusion policies

The **inclusion policy** sets the rule for which levels can hold the same line.

| Policy | Rule | Capacity | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inclusive | every line in L1 is also in L2 (and L3) | the largest level | Intel L3 from Nehalem to client Skylake |
| Non-inclusive (NINE) | no rule: each level fills and removes lines on its own | between the two | Intel Skylake-SP and later server L3 |
| Exclusive | each line is in exactly one level | the sum of the levels | AMD Zen L3, a victim cache for L2 |

A **victim cache** holds the lines that the level above it removes. When an inclusive L2 removes a line, it must also remove the line from L1. This is a **back-invalidation**.

<InclusionStepperDemo />

1. Keep **Inclusive** and **Hot line H + a stream**. The program uses H between the lines of a stream. L1 keeps H, but the L1 hits do not reach L2.
2. Watch L2. H moves to the end of the LRU order in L2, and L2 removes it. Inclusion then removes H from L1 too. H misses on 25% of its accesses.
3. Select **Non-inclusive** or **Exclusive**. H stays in L1 and does not miss again after its first access.
4. Select **Loop over 6 lines**. Inclusive and non-inclusive hold only 4 different lines, so every access goes to memory.
5. Keep that loop and select **Exclusive**. The hierarchy holds 2 + 4 = 6 lines, and after the first pass every access hits in L2.

The advantage of inclusion is **coherence**, which keeps the copies of a line in different caches the same. If L3 includes everything, another core must check only L3 to know if a private cache holds a line. So the L3 also works as a **snoop filter**. The costs are duplicated capacity and the problem below.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Back-invalidation removes hot lines">
L1 hits never reach L2, so the replacement policy of L2 does not see that a line is hot. A streaming scan pushes the hot line to the LRU end of L2. L2 removes it, and inclusion forces L1 to remove it too. In the first demo, a 2 KiB hot set misses L1 on 12.6% of its accesses under the inclusive policy, and never under the others. Jaleel and others measured this on real workloads (MICRO 2010). They proposed hints that tell L2 which lines are hot.
</Callout>

Exclusive hierarchies prevent the duplication. But every L2 hit swaps lines between the levels, and L1 must write each victim down to L2. This adds traffic between the levels. For this reason, exclusive designs are usually "mostly exclusive" victim caches.

## Using this

- **Know your sizes**: use `lscpu`, `getconf -a | grep CACHE`, or `/sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cache/`. Typical values today are 32 to 48 KiB of L1D, 1 to 2 MiB of L2 per core, and a few MiB of shared L3 per core.
- **Size working sets to a level**: fit tiles to L1 or L2, and hash tables to L2 or L3. The steps in the plot are the budget for your design.
- **A shared L3 has noisy neighbors**: a streaming job on another core can remove your working set. Cache partitioning (Intel CAT, AMD QoS) exists for this problem.
