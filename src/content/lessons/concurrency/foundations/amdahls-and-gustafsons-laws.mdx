The **speedup** on P cores is the time on one core divided by the time on P cores. Ideal speedup is P. Real programs fall short for three reasons: work that only one core can do, extra work that parallelism adds, and waits between cores. This lesson derives the classic laws for the first reason and measures all three on the shared runtime of this track.

The [contention](/performance/concurrency/contention) lesson states Amdahl's formula and plots it. Here the speedup comes from simulated runs, so you can see when the formula holds and when it does not.

## Amdahl's law

Gene Amdahl presented the argument at the AFIPS Spring Joint Computer Conference in 1967. Let the time on one core be 1, and let a fraction s of it be serial.

<Steps>
<div><b>One core:</b> $T(1) = s + (1 - s) = 1$.</div>
<div><b>P cores:</b> the serial part still takes s. The parallel part divides evenly, so $T(P) = s + (1 - s)/P$.</div>
<div><b>Speedup:</b> $S(P) = 1 / (s + (1 - s)/P)$.</div>
<div><b>Limit:</b> as P grows, $(1 - s)/P$ goes to 0, so $S \to 1/s$. With s = 10%, no number of cores gives more than 10.</div>
<div><b>Half of the limit:</b> $S(P) = P/2$ when $s(P - 1) = 1$. With s = 10%, 11 cores give a speedup of 5.5, and half of every core is lost.</div>
</Steps>

The law assumes that the serial part runs alone. A serial phase, such as reading the input or merging the results, fits that assumption. A lock does not fit it exactly. Other threads keep working while one thread holds the lock, so the curve rises faster and stops at the same limit 1/s. The limit holds because the lock can serve at most 1/s threads' worth of updates.

## Gustafson's law

John Gustafson argued in "Reevaluating Amdahl's Law" (Communications of the ACM, 1988) that people do not run a fixed problem on a bigger machine. They run a bigger problem in the same time.

<Steps>
<div><b>Measure on P cores:</b> let the run take 1, and let a fraction s′ of that time be serial.</div>
<div><b>The same work on one core:</b> the serial part takes s′, and the parallel part takes P times longer: $s' + P(1 - s')$.</div>
<div><b>Scaled speedup:</b> $S = s' + P(1 - s') = P - s'(P - 1)$. It grows without a limit.</div>
</Steps>

The two laws do not disagree. They define the serial fraction differently. Amdahl's s is a share of the one-core time, and Gustafson's s′ is a share of the P-core time. A run with s′ = 10% on 64 cores has a scaled speedup of 57.7. For that grown problem, the serial share of the one-core time is only 0.17%.

| | Strong scaling | Weak scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Problem size | Fixed | Grows with the core count |
| Question | How much sooner is one job done? | How much more work in the same time? |
| Law | Amdahl | Gustafson |
| Examples | Build one binary, render one frame, answer one query | Finer simulation grids, more data per node, more requests per server |

## Find the serial fraction in your measurements

Alan Karp and Horace Flatt (Communications of the ACM, 1990) inverted Amdahl's law. From a measured speedup S on p cores, the **Karp-Flatt metric** is the serial fraction that would explain it:

$$
e = \frac{1/S - 1/p}{1 - 1/p}
$$

If e stays constant as p grows, the program has a fixed serial part. If e grows with p, something else costs more as p grows: communication, synchronization or load imbalance. In the first demo, the phase program gives e = 0.100 at every core count. The locked program without costs gives 0.090 at 64 cores, because the lock overlaps with parallel work.

## When throughput falls: the Universal Scalability Law

Neil Gunther presented the **Universal Scalability Law** (USL) in 1993. It adds a second overhead to Amdahl's law:

$$
C(N) = \frac{N}{1 + \sigma (N - 1) + \kappa N (N - 1)}
$$

C(N) is the throughput with N workers, relative to one worker. The **contention** σ is the share that waits for a shared resource, and it is Amdahl's s. The **coherency** κ is the cost of making the workers agree. That cost grows with the number of pairs of workers, so the term grows as N(N − 1). With κ > 0, the curve has a peak at $N^* = \sqrt{(1 - \sigma)/\kappa}$, and throughput falls after it.

1. Read the dots. Throughput peaks at 8.88 times one thread, at 12 threads, and then falls. The USL fit gives σ = 0.0011, κ = 0.0034 and a peak at 17.2 threads.
2. The dashed line keeps the fitted σ and sets κ = 0. Amdahl's law cannot draw a curve that falls.
3. Read the Karp-Flatt plot. The value e rises from 0.017 at 8 threads to 0.197 at 64. A rising e is the sign of an overhead that grows with the thread count.
4. Set **Extra lock time per other thread** to 0. The dots now rise to a ceiling of 11.45 and stay flat. The fit still finds a small κ, because a full lock makes a sharper corner than the USL can draw. A fit summarizes the data, and it does not prove a cause.
5. Set **Work inside the lock** to 200 ns. The ceiling drops to 6.79.

What causes κ in real systems? Every cache line that all cores write, such as a shared counter or a lock word, moves between the cores on each write. A broadcast or a global barrier makes each worker wait for every other worker. The [false sharing](/performance/concurrency/false-sharing) lesson shows one source that is easy to miss.

## Using the laws

- **Measure throughput at several core counts**, and include some past the count you plan to use. Compute e or fit the USL. Two points cannot show a trend.
- **Reduce σ** with shorter critical sections, sharded data, batching, or work outside the lock. The [lock contention](/concurrency/locks/lock-contention) lesson measures these.
- **Reduce κ** by removing writes that every core shares, and by replacing all-to-all communication with local work and occasional merges.
- **Remember the constants.** The formulas ignore spawn, join, wake-up and switch costs. The third step of the first demo shows that these costs can matter more than the serial fraction.
- **Choose the right question.** For the latency of one job, use strong scaling. For the throughput of a service, the load usually grows with the machine, which is weak scaling, until σ and κ stop it.
