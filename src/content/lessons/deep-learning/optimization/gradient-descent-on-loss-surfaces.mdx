To train a model, you make its **loss** small. The loss is one number that measures the error of the model. The model has parameters $\theta$. The **gradient** $\nabla L(\theta)$ points in the direction where the loss increases fastest. Gradient descent moves a small step in the opposite direction:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L(\theta)$$

The **learning rate** $\eta$ sets the size of each step. Real models have millions of parameters. But almost all problems of this update also occur with two parameters. These problems are oscillation, divergence, slow progress in flat directions, saddle points, and different end points. This lesson shows each problem in 2D. Then it calculates the numbers that are also true in high dimensions.

The bowl is **ill-conditioned**: it is 20 times more curved in y than in x. In the demo, SGD is the plain update above. (SGD means stochastic gradient descent. It is "stochastic" when the gradient has noise.) Things to try on the bowl:

1. Turn off momentum and Adam. With SGD at η = 0.09, the path zig-zags across the narrow valley. It moves slowly along the long valley.
2. Move the learning rate just above **0.1**. SGD diverges, and its curve in the lower plot goes up.
3. Turn momentum on and set β to 0.5. Momentum gets the loss gap below $10^{-6}$ in 27 steps. SGD needs 86 steps.
4. Set β to 0.99. Momentum now circles the minimum for a long time. It has too much inertia.

Then change the surface:

- **Rosenbrock valley**: a curved valley. Lower the learning rate to about 0.01 first. All methods find the valley quickly. Then they move slowly along it.
- **Saddle point**: SGD starts almost on the ridge, at y = 0.01. At η = 0.09, it stays near the ridge for 55 steps before it rolls off. Add **gradient noise**, and it leaves sooner.
- **Himmelblau**: this surface has four minima. Lower the learning rate first, because the gradients are large. Then click different start points. The minimum where you end depends on the **basin** of the start: the area that drains to that minimum.

## The quadratic model

Near a minimum, a smooth loss is almost a quadratic: $L(\theta) \approx L^* + \tfrac12(\theta - \theta^*)^\top H\,(\theta - \theta^*)$. Here $H$ is the **Hessian**, the matrix of second derivatives. It gives the curvature in each direction. If you understand quadratics, you understand the last part of every optimization.

<Steps>
<div>The gradient of the quadratic is $H(\theta - \theta^*)$. Let $e = \theta - \theta^*$ be the error. Then the update is $e_{k+1} = (I - \eta H)\,e_k$.</div>
<div>Use the eigenvectors of $H$ as the axes. The **eigenvalues** $\lambda_i$ are the curvatures along these axes. Each coordinate then changes alone: $e^{(i)}_{k+1} = (1 - \eta\lambda_i)\,e^{(i)}_k$.</div>
<div><b>Stability</b> needs $|1 - \eta\lambda_i| < 1$ for every $i$. So $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$. The *sharpest* direction sets the limit.</div>
<div><b>Speed</b> depends on the slowest factor. The best fixed step is $\eta = 2/(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min})$. It gives the factor $\dfrac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}$ per step. Here $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the <b>condition number</b>.</div>
</Steps>

For the bowl, κ = 20. So the best factor is 19/21 ≈ 0.905 per step. The error then needs about 23 steps to become 10 times smaller. A step that is large enough for the flat direction is too large for the steep direction. **Ill-conditioning, not the shape of a non-convex surface, is the usual reason for slow training.**

<Callout title="The same instability as Euler">
Gradient descent is explicit Euler applied to the gradient flow $\dot\theta = -\nabla L$. The limit $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$ is the same limit as $\Delta t < 2\tau$ in the RC circuit lesson, with $\tau = 1/\lambda$.
</Callout>

## Momentum

The heavy-ball method keeps a velocity $v$: $v \leftarrow \beta v + \nabla L$, then $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta v$. The **momentum coefficient** $\beta$ sets how much of the old velocity stays.

- In the steep direction, the gradient changes sign at each step. These gradients cancel in $v$, so the zig-zag becomes smaller.
- In the flat direction, the gradient keeps the same sign. These gradients add up in $v$, so the step grows to almost $\eta/(1-\beta)$.

With the best $\eta$ and $\beta$, the factor per step becomes $\dfrac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}$. For the bowl, the best values are η ≈ 0.13 and β ≈ 0.40. The factor is then 0.63 instead of 0.905. So the error becomes 10 times smaller in about 5 steps, not 23 steps. For $\kappa = 10^4$, the difference is about 115 steps against about 11,500 steps.

Momentum is not always faster. A β that is too high makes the ball overshoot and circle. On the bowl at η = 0.09, β = 0.9 needs 170 steps, and β = 0.99 does not converge in 400 steps.

## Adam

Adam keeps a running average of the gradient ($m$) and of the squared gradient ($s$). It steps by $\eta\,\hat m / (\sqrt{\hat s} + \epsilon)$:

- **Scale for each coordinate.** Adam divides by the RMS (root mean square) gradient of each coordinate. So the step is about $\eta$ in *every* coordinate, for all curvatures. On the bowl, the axes of the bowl are the coordinate axes. So Adam removes the bad conditioning and moves almost straight to the minimum.
- **Bias correction.** $\hat m = m/(1-\beta_1^t)$ and $\hat s = s/(1-\beta_2^t)$. These corrections remove the effect of the zero start values in the first steps.
- **What Adam does not fix.** The scale is diagonal: one number per coordinate. So Adam corrects only part of a curvature that is not on the axes, such as the curved Rosenbrock valley. With noisy gradients, the steps of Adam do not become small near the minimum. So Adam moves around the minimum and does not settle. This is one reason why training decreases the learning rate over time.

## Noise, saddles and basins

A **minibatch** is a small random part of the training data. A minibatch gradient is the true gradient plus noise. The variance of this noise is proportional to $1/B$, where $B$ is the batch size. The CLT lesson explains why.

- **With a constant learning rate, SGD does not converge.** It stays in a cloud around the minimum, and the size of the cloud grows with $\eta\sigma^2$. On the bowl with noise σ = 1, the mean loss gap is 0.25 at η = 0.09 and 0.005 at η = 0.01. Schedules that decrease $\eta$, such as cosine decay, make the cloud smaller.
- **Saddles are common in high dimensions.** A **saddle point** is a flat point that curves up in some directions and down in others. A minimum needs a positive curvature in *all* of millions of directions. So most flat points are saddles. Exactly on a saddle, the gradient points along the ridge. The distance from the ridge grows only like $(1 + \eta|\lambda|)^k$. Noise gives a first push away from the ridge.
- **The minimum that you reach depends on the start and on the step size.** A large learning rate cannot stay in a minimum with a curvature above $2/\eta$. So training prefers flat minima. This *implicit bias* is linked to better generalization. Training often stays exactly at this limit. This effect is the "edge of stability".
