Clocks drift apart, so machines must correct them again and again. A machine with a good clock, a **time server**, tells the others the time. But the answer travels over a network, and the network adds a delay that nobody can measure exactly. This lesson derives what a client can learn from one exchange of messages, and how large its error can be.

## Cristian's algorithm

Flaviu Cristian described the simplest method in "Probabilistic clock synchronization" (1989). The client notes its time $T_0$ and asks the server. The server replies with its time $S$. The client receives the reply at $T_1$ on its own clock. It assumes that the reply took half of the round trip, and it sets its clock to:

$$
S + \frac{T_1 - T_0}{2}
$$

For example, a request at 100 ms, a server time of 500 ms and a reply at 120 ms give 510 ms. The guess is good when the round trip is short. Cristian called the method probabilistic: a client keeps only the readings with a short round trip, so it can ask again until it gets one.

## NTP's four timestamps

The Network Time Protocol (NTP, RFC 5905) improves this in one way. The server stamps both the arrival and the departure of the request, so its own processing time does not count as network delay. One exchange gives four timestamps:

- $t_0$: the client sends the request, read on the client's clock.
- $t_1$: the server receives it, read on the server's clock.
- $t_2$: the server sends the reply, read on the server's clock.
- $t_3$: the client receives the reply, read on the client's clock.

Let $\theta$ be the amount to add to the client's clock, and let the paths take $d_{\text{out}}$ and $d_{\text{back}}$. Then $t_1 = t_0 + \theta + d_{\text{out}}$ and $t_3 = t_2 - \theta + d_{\text{back}}$. Two equations have three unknowns, so the client assumes $d_{\text{out}} = d_{\text{back}}$. This gives the two NTP formulas:

$$
\theta = \frac{(t_1 - t_0) + (t_2 - t_3)}{2} \qquad \delta = (t_3 - t_0) - (t_2 - t_1)
$$

## The error bound

Put the assumption in and look at what is left. The estimate is wrong by exactly half the asymmetry:

$$
\theta_{\text{est}} - \theta = \frac{d_{\text{out}} - d_{\text{back}}}{2}
$$

The client does not know the asymmetry, but it knows that each path is at least zero. So the worst case is a path of length $\delta$ in one direction and zero in the other, and $|\text{error}| \le \delta / 2$. This bound is always true, and it is the only one that the client can prove. It is also why NTP prefers samples with a small delay: a small $\delta$ gives a small bound.

Real paths are rarely symmetric. Routes can differ in each direction, and a queue in a busy switch adds delay to one direction only. The demo below runs 200 exchanges with random queueing on each path. Plotted as error against delay, the points fill a wedge.

<TimeNtpWedgeDemo />

1. Look at the shape. All points lie inside the dashed lines, where the error is at most $(\delta - \delta_{\min}) / 2$. Points with a large delay can have a large error.
2. Read the stats. With a mean queueing of 8 ms per direction (a model input), the mean error of a single sample is 3.86 ms. The filter picks have a mean error of 1.00 ms.
3. Set the queueing to 0.5 ms. The wedge gets narrow, and every sample is good.

The picks are NTP's **clock filter**. It keeps the last 8 samples from each server and uses the one with the lowest delay. NTP then combines several servers. It discards servers whose intervals do not overlap with the majority (the **falsetickers**). It drops outliers from the rest and takes a weighted average of the survivors.

## Strata, polling and slewing

NTP servers form layers called **strata**. A stratum 1 server reads a reference clock directly, such as GPS or an atomic clock. A stratum 2 server synchronizes to stratum 1, and so on. RFC 5905 uses stratum 16 to mean "unsynchronized". The client asks again at a **poll interval**, a power of two in seconds. The RFC suggests 64 s to 1024 s as default limits.

Between two polls, the clock drifts freely. So the error has a saw-tooth shape.

<TimeNtpSyncDemo />

1. With the defaults (drift 50 ppm, poll every 64 s), the clock walks 3.2 ms between polls. The largest error after the first poll is 4.5 ms, because each correction also has a small error.
2. Set the poll interval to 1024 s. The walk grows to 51.2 ms. Polling less often costs accuracy in direct proportion.
3. Turn on **Correct the rate too**. The client estimates its drift from two corrections and cancels it, so the saw-tooth becomes flat. Real NTP disciplines the frequency in this way.
4. Set the queueing to 10 ms, and turn on **Best of 8**. The filter removes most of the noise that the queueing adds.

How does a daemon apply a correction? It can **step** the clock (jump to the new value) or **slew** it (run it fast or slow until it catches up). The ntpd documentation says that it steps when the offset is above a threshold of 128 ms, and it slews below that. It also says that typical Unix kernels limit the slew to 0.5 ms per second, so each second of error needs 2,000 s to remove. Slewing keeps the time-of-day clock monotonic, which is why daemons prefer it.

## PTP and what accuracy to expect

The **Precision Time Protocol** (PTP, IEEE 1588) uses the same four-timestamp idea. Network cards stamp packets in hardware, which removes the delay of the software stack. Switches that support PTP can measure the time a packet spends inside them and correct for it. On a local network that is built for it, PTP can be much more accurate than NTP over the internet.

The numbers depend on the network, the hardware and the load. Treat these as model inputs, not as promises:

| Setup (model input) | Typical skew to plan for |
|---|---|
| NTP over the public internet | milliseconds to tens of milliseconds |
| NTP in one data center | sub-millisecond to a few milliseconds |
| PTP with hardware timestamps | sub-microsecond to microseconds |

## Why a bound matters more than the error

A system can not use a clock safely if it does not know the clock's error. With a bound, it can. If every clock is within $\varepsilon$ of true time, then two events whose timestamps differ by more than $2\varepsilon$ are surely in that order. This is the idea behind Google's TrueTime, which returns an interval and not a single time.

The Spanner paper (OSDI 2012) gives concrete numbers. Each machine assumes a worst-case drift of 200 µs per second between polls of its time masters. The uncertainty grows from 0 to 6 ms between polls, and 1 ms more comes from the communication delay. Spanner waits out the uncertainty before it makes a commit visible.

There is one danger. A bound is an assumption, and a broken assumption gives wrong answers with no error. A clock with a bad crystal, or a virtual machine that was paused, can break it. So systems that rely on a bound also check it, and they stop a node whose clock is out of range.
