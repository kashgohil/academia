A resistor and a capacitor in series make the simplest circuit with *memory*. Its output now depends on what happened before. A **capacitor** stores electric charge $q$, and its voltage is $v_C = q/C$. The **capacitance** $C$ sets how much charge it stores for each volt. You find this circuit in switch debouncing, power-on reset, timers, filters, sample-and-hold circuits, and the delay of every wire on a chip.

Things to try:

1. Turn on **Time axis in units of τ**. Now all values of R, C and Vs give the *same* curve. The circuit has one timescale of its own.
2. Select **Discharge**. The capacitor becomes the source, and the current changes sign.
3. Look at the current plot. At t = 0, the current jumps to Vs/R. An empty capacitor first acts like a wire: a **short circuit**.
4. Wait until the curve is flat. The current is now zero. A full capacitor acts like a break in the wire: an **open circuit**.

## Why the curve is exponential

Think of the capacitor as a bucket of water. The water level is the voltage $v_C$. The source is a large tank at the level $V_s$. A narrow pipe, the resistor, connects the tank and the bucket. The flow in the pipe is the current, and it is proportional to the difference in level.

<RcBucketDemo />

1. Press **Step** a few times. Each step adds the same fraction of the remaining gap. The gap is large at the start, so the first steps add much water.
2. Press **Play**. The steps become smaller as the gap closes. The dots follow the exponential curve. With Δt/τ = 0.2, the level after 5 steps (t = τ) is 0.672. The exact value is 0.632.
3. Set the step size to **0.05**. The level after 20 steps (again t = τ) is 0.642. Smaller steps come closer to the exact curve.
4. Select **Fixed flow**. A current source gives a constant flow. Now the level rises in a straight line and reaches Vs at t = τ. This line is the tangent of the exponential curve at t = 0.

The rule "add a fixed fraction of the gap" gives a curve that never reaches the top. This rule is also the explicit Euler method, which the last section examines.

## The differential equation

<Steps>
<div>Kirchhoff's voltage law says that the voltages around the loop add to the source voltage: $V_s = v_R + v_C = iR + v_C$.</div>
<div>The capacitor stores $q = Cv_C$. The current is the rate of change of the charge, so $i = C\,\dfrac{dv_C}{dt}$.</div>
<div>Put this into the first equation: $RC\,\dfrac{dv_C}{dt} + v_C = V_s$. This is a linear differential equation of first order. Its **time constant** is $\tau = RC$.</div>
<div>Let $e = V_s - v_C$ be the *remaining gap*. Then $\dfrac{de}{dt} = -\dfrac{e}{\tau}$. The gap decreases at a rate that is proportional to the gap.</div>
<div>So $e(t) = e(0)\,e^{-t/\tau}$. With $v_C(0) = 0$: $$v_C(t) = V_s\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad i(t) = \frac{V_s}{R}e^{-t/\tau}$$</div>
</Steps>

Step 4 contains the main idea. *Each* quantity whose rate of change is proportional to its distance from equilibrium decays exponentially. Examples are Newton's law of cooling, radioactive decay, a first-order low-pass filter, and an exponential moving average. When you see this structure, you know the answer.

| Time | Charged to | Remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 1τ | 63.2% | 36.8% |
| 2τ | 86.5% | 13.5% |
| 3τ | 95.0% | 5.0% |
| 5τ | 99.3% | 0.7% |

Engineers use **5τ as "fully charged"**. In the mathematics, the capacitor never becomes fully charged.

<Callout title="Units check">
Ω × F = (V/A) × (C/V) = C/A = s. A 10 kΩ resistor with 100 µF gives τ = 1 s. The same resistor with 100 pF gives τ = 1 µs.
</Callout>

## Where the energy goes

The source moves the charge $Q = CV_s$ at the constant voltage $V_s$. So the source supplies the energy $QV_s = CV_s^2$. The capacitor stores only $\tfrac12 CV_s^2$. **The resistor changes exactly half of the energy into heat, for all values of R.** A smaller R gives a larger current for a shorter time, and the total heat stays the same.

For example, with C = 100 µF and Vs = 5 V, the source supplies 2.5 mJ. The capacitor stores 1.25 mJ, and the resistor makes 1.25 mJ of heat. So when a fixed voltage source charges a capacitor, half the energy is always lost. This loss is also part of the CMOS dynamic power, $P \approx \alpha C V^2 f$. Each switching event loses that $\tfrac12 CV^2$.

## The same circuit as a filter

Connect a square wave instead of a single step. The capacitor voltage is the output. The **period** $P$ is the time for one full cycle of the input.

>
<RcSquareWaveDemo />
</Predict>

1. Start at **τ / period = 0.1**. Each half period lasts 5τ, so the capacitor almost reaches each level. The output swing is 98.7% of Vs.
2. Move to **1**. The edges become round curves. The swing decreases to 24.5% of Vs.
3. Move to **10**. The output stays near Vs/2, and the swing is only 2.5%. The circuit now gives the *average* of the input.

In steady state, let $a = e^{-P/(2\tau)}$. The output swings between $V_s\,a/(1+a)$ and $V_s/(1+a)$. So the swing is $V_s\tanh\!\big(P/(4\tau)\big)$, and the average is always $V_s/2$.

This is a **low-pass filter**: slow changes pass, and fast changes become small. A sine wave input shows the same effect. Frequencies below the **corner frequency** $f_c = \dfrac{1}{2\pi RC}$ pass, and higher frequencies decrease. At τ = P, the square wave frequency is 6.28 times $f_c$. The step response and the frequency response are two views of the same circuit. The AC module develops the second view in the lessons on impedance and passive filters.

## Simulating it: where numerical methods fail

A simulator replaces the derivative with a finite step $\Delta t$. Explicit (forward) Euler calculates $v_{k+1} = v_k + \frac{\Delta t}{\tau}(V_s - v_k)$. This is the bucket rule from the second demo.

<Predict
  id="euler"
  question="You simulate this circuit with explicit Euler and a time step Δt = 2.2τ. What does the simulated voltage do?"
  options={["It converges to Vs, but less accurately", "It converges, but it overshoots and rings first", "It oscillates with a growing amplitude and blows up", "It stays at 0"]}
  answer={2}
  explain="Each step multiplies the error by (1 − Δt/τ). For Δt > 2τ, this factor has a magnitude above 1. So the error grows without limit, but the real circuit is fully stable."
>
<RcEulerDemo />
</Predict>

Write the error as $e_k = V_s - v_k$. Then $e_{k+1} = (1 - \Delta t/\tau)\,e_k$. This gives three regimes:

- $\Delta t < \tau$: the factor is between 0 and 1. The error decreases smoothly, as in the real circuit.
- $\tau < \Delta t < 2\tau$: the factor is between −1 and 0. The error changes sign at each step, so the solution rings. The real circuit never rings.
- $\Delta t > 2\tau$: the error grows. This is **numerical instability**.

In a large circuit, the *fastest* time constant limits the step size. This can be a very small stray capacitance, even when only the slow behavior is important. This problem is **stiffness**. Because of stiffness, SPICE uses implicit integrators. The numerical methods module and the integrators module of the physics track give more detail.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Real components">
Electrolytic capacitors have a tolerance of ±20%, a leakage current, and an equivalent series resistance (ESR). Ceramic capacitors lose capacitance under DC bias. Do not design a precise timer that relies on the nominal RC value.
</Callout>
