The Internet is not one network. It is tens of thousands of **autonomous systems** (ASes). Each AS is a network with one operator and one routing policy, such as an ISP, a cloud provider or a university. Each AS has an AS number, 32 bits wide since RFC 4893 (2007), which RFC 6793 now replaces. Inside an AS, [OSPF or IS-IS](/networking/ip/routing-distance-vector-and-link-state) find the shortest paths. Between ASes, one protocol carries every route: the **Border Gateway Protocol**, version 4 (BGP, RFC 4271).

BGP is different from interior protocols in one important way. Its goal is not the shortest path. Its goal is the path that each AS's business allows and prefers. Money decides most routes on the Internet.

## Sessions and messages

Two BGP routers open a TCP connection to port 179 and exchange four kinds of messages:

- **OPEN** starts the session, with the AS number and a proposed **hold time**. RFC 4271 suggests 90 seconds.
- **KEEPALIVE** proves that the peer is alive, typically every third of the hold time.
- **UPDATE** announces routes to prefixes, with their attributes, or withdraws routes.
- **NOTIFICATION** reports an error and closes the session.

A session between two ASes is **eBGP**. Routers inside one AS also run BGP among themselves, called **iBGP**, so that every border router knows the external routes.

## Path vector

A BGP route carries the list of ASes it crossed, the `AS_PATH`. Each AS adds its own number to the front when it passes a route to a neighbour. A router rejects any route whose path already contains its own AS number. This one rule stops the loops that make distance-vector protocols [count to infinity](/networking/ip/routing-distance-vector-and-link-state): BGP is a **path-vector** protocol. The path also lets an AS write policy, such as "never send traffic through AS X".

## The decision process

A router often hears several routes to the same prefix. RFC 4271 fixes the order of comparison. The first rule that leaves exactly one route decides; later rules do not matter.

| Order | Rule | Who sets it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | highest `LOCAL_PREF` | this AS's own policy |
| 2 | shortest `AS_PATH` | the path; others can lengthen it by prepending |
| 3 | lowest `ORIGIN` (IGP, then EGP, then INCOMPLETE) | the AS that first announced it |
| 4 | lowest `MED`, only among routes from the same neighbour AS | the neighbour |
| 5 | eBGP over iBGP | the session type |
| 6 | lowest interior cost to the `NEXT_HOP` | the IGP |
| 7 | lowest BGP identifier, then lowest peer address | the final tie-breaks |

Vendors add their own steps; Cisco, for example, compares a local "weight" before everything else.

Prepending is how an AS asks the world to send less traffic over one link. It adds its own number several times to the routes it announces there. It is only a request. An AS that sets `LOCAL_PREF` on the route ignores it, because rule 1 comes first.

Rule 6 creates **hot-potato routing**. When two exits are otherwise equal, an AS hands the traffic to the neighbour at the exit closest to where it entered. Traffic then often takes different paths in the two directions.

## Policy: customers, peers and providers

Most links between ASes are one of two kinds. On a **customer-provider** link, the customer pays the provider to carry its traffic to and from the rest of the Internet. On a **peer** link, two ASes exchange traffic between their own customers without payment. Gao and Rexford (IEEE/ACM ToN, 2001) described the policy that follows from this:

- **Prefer** routes from customers (they pay you) over routes from peers (free) or providers (you pay). Most ASes also prefer peers over providers, but the proof does not need it. An AS sets this with `LOCAL_PREF`.
- **Export** routes from customers, and your own, to everybody. Export routes from peers and providers only to your customers. Nobody carries traffic between two parties that do not pay for it.

The resulting paths are **valley-free**: they climb from customer to provider, cross at most one peer link, and then only descend. Assume that every AS follows these rules and no AS is its own indirect provider. Gao and Rexford proved that BGP then always converges to a stable state. Without such rules, BGP policies can oscillate forever.

```go
// The Gao-Rexford export rule, as a filter on each outgoing route.
func mayExport(learnedFrom, to Relationship) bool {
	switch learnedFrom {
	case Self, Customer:
		return true // customers' routes go to everyone: they pay for this
	default: // Peer or Provider
		return to == Customer // never give free transit to a peer or provider
	}
}
```

## Hijacks and leaks

BGP believes what neighbours say. Nothing in RFC 4271 checks that an AS may announce a prefix. Two kinds of accident or attack follow:

- A **hijack**: an AS announces a prefix that belongs to someone else.
- A **route leak**: an AS passes on routes that its policy should keep, for example from one provider to another (RFC 7908).

Real incidents follow these patterns:

- **YouTube, 24 February 2008.** At 18:47 UTC, Pakistan Telecom (AS17557) announced 208.65.153.0/24, a part of YouTube's 208.65.152.0/22, and its provider PCCW (AS3491) passed it on. YouTube announced the same /24 at 20:07 and two /25s at 20:18. PCCW withdrew the bad route at 21:01 (RIPE NCC case study).
- **The 2019 leak through Verizon.** On 24 June 2019, a BGP optimizer at DQE Communications (AS33154) created more-specific routes. Its customer Allegheny Technologies (AS396531) leaked them to Verizon (AS701), which accepted them. Cloudflare lost about 15% of its global traffic at the worst point. Cloudflare's ROAs allowed nothing longer than a /20. Its post-mortem noted that ROV, a prefix limit or IRR filters at Verizon would have dropped the leaked routes.
- **Facebook, 4 October 2021.** A maintenance command took down Facebook's backbone. Its DNS servers are designed to withdraw their BGP routes when they cannot reach the data centers, and they did. The rest of the Internet could no longer find Facebook for about six hours.

## RPKI and route origin validation

The **Resource Public Key Infrastructure** (RPKI, RFC 6480) lets the holder of a prefix sign a **Route Origin Authorization** (ROA). A ROA says, for example: "AS6 may originate 198.51.100.0/22, with prefixes up to /22". Routers download the validated ROAs and check each route's origin AS (RFC 6811):

- **Valid**: a ROA covers the prefix, with this origin AS and a length within maxLength.
- **Invalid**: ROAs cover the prefix, but none matches the origin or the length.
- **NotFound**: no ROA covers the prefix.

An AS that does **route origin validation** (ROV) drops invalid routes. The demo shows its two limits. First, ROV protects a network only if the networks on its paths deploy it too. Second, it checks only the last AS in the path, so an attacker can forge the origin. A loose maxLength makes this easy, because then the forged /24 is valid. RFC 9319 (2022) therefore recommends avoiding maxLength in most cases.

BGPsec (RFC 8205) signs the whole path, but it needs every AS on the path to take part. For leaks, RFC 9234 (2022) adds BGP roles and an "Only to Customer" attribute. With it, a router can detect a route that has already gone down to a customer.

## Slow convergence

BGP converges slowly. A router waits a minimum time between updates for the same destination, the MinRouteAdvertisementInterval. RFC 4271 suggests 30 seconds for eBGP. After a withdrawal, routers also try every longer path they know before they give up, called **path exploration**. Labovitz and others measured this in "An Experimental Study of Delayed Internet Routing Convergence" (SIGCOMM 2000). Failover for a multihomed site took about three minutes on average, and some events took up to fifteen minutes.
