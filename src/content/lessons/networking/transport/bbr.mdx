Reno and CUBIC are **loss-based**: they grow the window until a queue overflows, and they treat the loss as the signal. The [previous lesson](/networking/transport/congestion-control-reno-and-cubic) showed two costs. A deep buffer fills, so every flow waits behind it. A random loss that has nothing to do with congestion cuts the window anyway.

BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time) takes a different approach. Cardwell, Cheng, Gunn, Hassas Yeganeh and Jacobson described it in ACM Queue in 2016. It arrived in Linux 4.9. BBR builds a model of the path from measurements and sends at the rate that the model gives. This lesson describes BBR version 1, the one in mainline Linux, and then what later versions change.

The runs here use the same packet model as the previous lesson: 1500-byte packets, one ACK per packet, a 200 ms minimum RTO. The BBR controller follows `tcp_bbr.c`, with the constants of that file.

## Two numbers describe a path

For one flow, a path has two properties that matter:

- **BtlBw**, the bottleneck bandwidth: the rate of the slowest link.
- **RTprop**, the round-trip propagation time: the RTT with empty queues.

Their product is the BDP. With less than one BDP in flight, the link idles part of each round trip. With more, the extra packets wait in a queue. They add delay but no throughput.

The best point is at one BDP: full rate and the least delay. A loss-based controller cannot stay there, because it gets no signal until the red line. BBR tries to work at the green line.

There is a catch. To see RTprop, the queue must be empty, so the flight must be at most one BDP. To see BtlBw, the link must be full, so the flight must be at least one BDP. No single measurement shows both. BBR therefore measures them at different times and keeps each in a filter.

## The two filters

- **BtlBw** is the maximum of the delivery-rate samples over the last 10 round trips. A **delivery-rate sample** is the data delivered between the send of a packet and its ACK, divided by that interval.
- **RTprop** is the minimum RTT sample over the last 10 seconds.

A maximum filter fits BtlBw because queues and ACK delays can only make a rate look lower. A minimum filter fits RTprop because queues can only make an RTT look longer. From the model, BBR sets two controls.

$$
\text{pacing rate} = g_{\text{pacing}} \cdot \text{BtlBw}, \qquad \text{cwnd} = g_{\text{cwnd}} \cdot \text{BtlBw} \cdot \text{RTprop}
$$

The pacing rate is the main control. The window is only a cap for when ACKs arrive late. BBR needs **pacing**: the sender spaces its packets in time instead of sending a burst per ACK. Before Linux 4.13 this needed the `fq` queueing discipline. Since 4.13, TCP falls back to pacing of its own.

```ts
// BBR's model update on each ACK (simplified from src/sims/net/cc/bbr.ts).
onAck(a: AckInfo) {
  if (a.rate.priorDelivered >= this.nextRoundDelivered) { // a new round trip starts
    this.nextRoundDelivered = a.delivered;
    this.round++;
  }
  this.btlBw = this.maxFilter.update(this.round, a.rate.deliveryRate); // max over 10 rounds
  if (a.rtt <= this.rtProp || a.now - this.rtStamp > 10_000) {        // min over 10 s
    this.rtProp = a.rtt;
    this.rtStamp = a.now;
  }
  this.pacingRate = this.pacingGain * this.btlBw;
  this.cwnd = this.cwndGain * this.btlBw * this.rtProp;
}
```

## The state machine

| State | Pacing gain | cwnd gain | Ends when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Startup | 2/ln 2 ≈ 2.89 | 2.89 | BtlBw grew less than 25% in 3 rounds in a row |
| Drain | 1/2.89 ≈ 0.35 | 2.89 | the flight is down to one BDP |
| ProbeBW | cycle 1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 | 2 | (it stays here) each phase lasts about one RTprop |
| ProbeRTT | 1 | cwnd = 4 packets | 200 ms and one round trip have passed |

Startup doubles the delivery rate each round, as slow start does. A gain of 2/ln 2 is the smallest gain that can double it each round. When three rounds bring less than 25% more, the pipe is full, and Drain removes the queue that Startup made.

In ProbeBW, the 1.25 phase sends a little more to discover new bandwidth. The 0.75 phase drains the queue that the probe made. Linux starts each flow at a random phase of the cycle, but never at the 0.75 phase. ProbeRTT starts when RTprop has had no new minimum for 10 seconds. The window then drops to 4 packets, so that the queue empties and a fresh RTprop appears.

2. Look at the thin pacing line in ProbeBW. It moves up by 25% for one RTT and down by 25% for the next. The rate stays at 10 Mbit/s, and the queue stays small.

4. Select **RTT to 120 ms**. RTprop keeps the old 41.5 ms value until ProbeRTT at 10.18 s. Until then the window cap is too small: from 8.5 to 10 s the flow gets only 6.5 Mbit/s.
5. Select **rate to 20 Mbit/s**. The 1.25 phases find the new bandwidth: BtlBw reaches 20 Mbit/s within 0.6 s.

## BBR in a deep buffer

Run one flow alone, as in the sawtooth demo of the previous lesson, with a buffer of 4 BDP. Reno and CUBIC fill it: their mean queueing delays are 108.5 ms and 92.1 ms. BBR keeps the mean at 9.8 ms with no drops at all, and its utilisation is 98.2%. This is BBR's main promise: full rate without bufferbloat.

## BBR and random loss

Loss is not part of BBR v1's model. A packet that is lost on a radio link or a bad cable does not change BtlBw or RTprop.

<NetCcBbrLossDemo />

1. At 0% both flows fill the 20 Mbit/s link.
2. At 1% CUBIC falls to 3.9 Mbit/s, close to the 3.7 Mbit/s of the Mathis formula. BBR keeps more than 19 Mbit/s.
3. At 10%, BBR still gets 10.2 Mbit/s and CUBIC gets 0.63 Mbit/s. At 20% BBR falls to 1.45 Mbit/s too, and 30% of what it sends are retransmissions.

This is why BBR helps on long paths with random loss. It is also BBR v1's main flaw, as the next section shows. Linux's v1 also has a special mode for traffic policers, which the model leaves out.

1. Set the buffer to 0.5 × BDP. BBR takes 9.02 Mbit/s and CUBIC 0.76 Mbit/s. CUBIC resends 14.8% of its packets. BBR overflows the small queue but does not back off, and CUBIC halves on each loss.
2. Set 2 × BDP. The two flows are close: 5.36 and 4.59 Mbit/s.
3. Set 8 × BDP. Now CUBIC wins, and the mean queueing delay is 173 ms. BBR's window cap of 2 BDP is the limit, not its pacing rate.

Testbed experiments agree. Hock, Bless and Zitterbart (ICNP 2017) found that BBR v1 causes heavy loss in buffers below about one BDP. In deep buffers they saw BBR limited by its in-flight cap, with a larger share for CUBIC as the buffer grows. Ware and co-authors (IMC 2019) found that BBR's share does not depend on the number of competing loss-based flows. One BBR flow took about 40% of the link against up to 16 CUBIC flows in deep buffers.

## BBRv2 and BBRv3

Google changed BBR twice to fix these problems. The IETF draft `draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr` (version 07, November 2026) describes BBRv3. It is an Internet-Draft that aims to become an Experimental RFC. Mainline Linux still has v1 in `tcp_bbr.c`; Google publishes its v3 code separately. The main changes in the draft:

| | BBR v1 (Linux) | BBRv3 (draft-07) |
|---|---|---|
| Loss | ignored by the model | a round with more than 2% loss sets an upper bound on the flight |
| In-flight bounds | 2 × BDP | adds `inflight_longterm` and `inflight_shortterm`, cut by β = 0.7 |
| Startup pacing gain | 2/ln 2 ≈ 2.89 | 4 ln 2 ≈ 2.77, cwnd gain 2 |
| Drain pacing gain | 1/2.89 | 0.5 |
| ProbeBW | 8 phases of one RTprop | DOWN (0.90), CRUISE (1.0), REFILL (1.0), UP (1.25) |
| ProbeRTT | cwnd 4 packets | cwnd 0.5 × BDP, 200 ms |

The draft also says that a sender that uses ECN must treat CE marks as congestion. It does not specify the response. The aim of these changes is to coexist with loss-based flows and to keep losses low in shallow buffers.

## Using BBR on Linux

```sh
sysctl -w net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control=bbr   # the default for new connections
sysctl -w net.core.default_qdisc=fq              # pacing in the qdisc; optional since Linux 4.13
ss -ti dst example.com                           # shows "bbr" and its bw and mrtt estimates
```

BBR is a sender-side change. A server can use BBR for its downloads while every client keeps CUBIC for its uploads.
