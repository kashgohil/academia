The network may lose, delay, duplicate or reorder a packet. A reliable transport must still deliver every byte once and in order. Protocols that do this with acknowledgments and retransmissions are called **ARQ** (automatic repeat request) protocols. They have four parts: sequence numbers, acknowledgments, a timer, and a rule that says what to send again.

## From stop-and-wait to a window

The simplest ARQ sends one segment and waits for its ACK. On a path with a 40 ms round trip, that is at most one segment per 40 ms, whatever the link rate. The fix is a **window**: the sender may have up to W segments sent and not yet acknowledged. A window of W segments sends W segments per round trip. To fill a path, W must reach the [bandwidth-delay product](/networking/foundations/latency-bandwidth-and-bdp).

With many segments in flight, a loss raises a new question: what does the sender resend? There are two textbook answers.

- **Go-back-N.** The receiver accepts only the next segment in order and drops anything after a gap. Its ACK is **cumulative**: "I have everything before n". On a timeout the sender resends the oldest segment and everything after it.
- **Selective repeat.** The receiver buffers segments after a gap. It acknowledges each segment on its own, and the sender keeps one timer per segment. On a timeout the sender resends only that segment.

TCP mixes the two. Its ACKs are cumulative, like go-back-N. Its receiver keeps out-of-order data, like selective repeat. On top of that, TCP finds most losses without waiting for a timer.

## Duplicate ACKs and fast retransmit

When segment 3 is lost, segments 4, 5 and 6 still arrive. Each one makes the receiver send an ACK that still asks for 3. Such an ACK, with no data and no new information, is a **duplicate ACK**. RFC 5681 lets a sender treat three duplicate ACKs as a loss.

The sender then resends segment 3 at once: **fast retransmit**. Why three and not one? The network can reorder packets, and one or two duplicates are often just reordering.

Without SACK, a sender in recovery uses **partial ACKs** (NewReno, RFC 6582). It records `recover`, the highest sequence number sent when the loss was found. An ACK that moves forward but stays below `recover` shows the next hole, and the sender resends that segment at once. The cost is one round trip per hole.

## SACK

**Selective acknowledgment** (SACK, RFC 2018) adds a TCP option to the ACK. It lists up to four blocks of data that arrived above the gap, or three when the timestamp option also takes space. Both sides must agree with the "SACK permitted" option in their SYNs.

The sender keeps a **scoreboard** of which segments are SACKed. RFC 6675 marks a segment as lost when enough data above it is SACKed. That is three separate SACKed pieces, or more than two full segments of bytes. It counts bytes in flight as a value called **pipe**, so that the sender can resend every hole within one round trip.

Linux has moved beyond counting duplicates. Its default loss detection is RACK-TLP (RFC 8985, 2021; `net.ipv4.tcp_recovery` = 1). RACK marks a segment lost when a segment sent later was delivered, plus a small time allowance for reordering. TLP sends a **tail loss probe** after about two round trips without an ACK. The probe makes the receiver send a SACK, so a tail loss is repaired without the full timeout.

## The retransmission timeout

When no ACK comes, only a timer helps. Its value is the **retransmission timeout** (RTO). Too short, and the sender resends segments that were only late. Too long, and the connection sits idle after a loss. RFC 6298 sets the RTO from two smoothed values: the RTT itself and its variation.

For the first sample R, the sender sets $\text{SRTT} = R$ and $\text{RTTVAR} = R/2$. For each later sample $R'$:

$$
\text{RTTVAR} \leftarrow (1-\beta)\,\text{RTTVAR} + \beta\,\lvert \text{SRTT} - R' \rvert, \qquad \text{SRTT} \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,\text{SRTT} + \alpha R'
$$

$$
\text{RTO} = \text{SRTT} + \max(G,\ 4\,\text{RTTVAR})
$$

Here $\alpha = 1/8$, $\beta = 1/4$, and G is the clock granularity. Before the first sample, the RTO is 1 s. RFC 6298 says the RTO SHOULD be at least 1 s, and any maximum must be at least 60 s. Linux applies its 200 ms minimum (`TCP_RTO_MIN`) to the variance term, so its RTO is at least SRTT + 200 ms. Its ceiling is 120 s (`TCP_RTO_MAX`).

Two more rules matter. **Karn's algorithm**: never take an RTT sample from a retransmitted segment, because the ACK might be for either copy. And after a timeout, the RTO **doubles** (exponential backoff), and it stays doubled until a new valid sample arrives. In the RFC 6298 model, a lost SYN therefore costs 1 s, then 2 s more, as the [handshake lesson](/networking/transport/tcp-handshake-and-teardown) shows.

```rust
// RFC 6298 in a few lines. Times in ms.
struct Rto { srtt: Option<f64>, rttvar: f64, rto: f64, backoffs: u32 }

impl Rto {
    fn on_sample(&mut self, r: f64) {
        match self.srtt {
            None => { self.srtt = Some(r); self.rttvar = r / 2.0; }
            Some(s) => {
                self.rttvar = 0.75 * self.rttvar + 0.25 * (s - r).abs();
                self.srtt = Some(0.875 * s + 0.125 * r);
            }
        }
        self.backoffs = 0;
        self.rto = (self.srtt.unwrap() + (4.0 * self.rttvar).max(1.0)).clamp(200.0, 120_000.0);
    }
    fn timer_value(&self) -> f64 { (self.rto * 2f64.powi(self.backoffs as i32)).min(120_000.0) }
    fn on_timeout(&mut self) { self.backoffs += 1; }
}
```

After an RTO, Linux marks the unacknowledged segments as lost, except recent ones that RACK still gives time. It sends them again under a new congestion window. Without SACK, that behaves like go-back-N. With SACK, segments that the receiver already reported are skipped.

Things to try:

1. Read the curves at 0% loss. All four protocols take the same time: the window and the round trip set it.
2. Switch to **copies sent per segment**. At 10% loss, go-back-N sends 2.01 copies of each segment. TCP with SACK sends 1.17, selective repeat 1.26, and TCP without SACK 1.44.
3. Compare the times at 10%: go-back-N needs 2.6 s for 60 segments, and TCP with SACK 1.3 s. Selective repeat sends few extra copies but waits for a timer at every loss.

The sweep shows the trade-off. Go-back-N keeps the receiver simple and wastes the link. Selective repeat saves the link and waits for timers. TCP with SACK gets both: it repairs most losses within a round trip and resends almost only what was lost. The ACKs here also cross a lossy path, and cumulative ACKs help: a later ACK covers the work of a lost one.
