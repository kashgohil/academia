[STCF](/os/scheduling/fifo-sjf-round-robin) gives short jobs good turnaround, and round-robin gives good response. STCF needs an oracle that does not exist. A **multilevel feedback queue** (MLFQ) replaces the oracle with history. It assumes that a job will behave as it has behaved so far. Corbató and his group built the first one for CTSS in 1962.

An MLFQ has several ready queues, each with a priority level. A job's behavior moves it between queues, and this is the "feedback".

## The rules

| Rule | Statement |
|---|---|
| 1 | If the priority of A is higher than the priority of B, A runs. |
| 2 | If the priorities are equal, A and B run in round-robin with the quantum of that level. |
| 3 | A new job enters at the highest priority. |
| 4 | When a job uses up its allotment at a level, it moves down one level. The number of times it gave up the CPU does not matter. |
| 5 | After each period $S$, all jobs move to the highest priority. |

The **allotment** is the CPU time that a job may use at one level. This table is the final form from the book OSTEP (*Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces*). Each rule fixes a failure of a simpler rule.

The first attempt had a different rule 4. **Rule 4a**: a job that uses a whole time slice moves down. **Rule 4b**: a job that gives up the CPU before the slice ends stays at its level. This treats interactive jobs well, but it has three failures:

- **Starvation.** Enough interactive jobs keep the top levels busy. A long job then gets no CPU.
- **Gaming.** A job can yield just before its slice ends, and it stays at the top for ever.
- **Change of behavior.** A CPU-bound job that becomes interactive stays at the bottom with no way up.

Rule 5 fixes the first and the third. The new rule 4 fixes the second.

1. Keep the yield point at 99%. The red curve stays near 98%, and the blue curve falls to about 50%.
2. Read the levels. Under the old rules the gamer ends in Q0, and under rule 4 it ends in Q2.
3. Move the yield point to 50%. With rule 4 the gamer's share falls to 34%. It uses less per turn, and it gains nothing from the yield.

Rule 4 only needs one change: keep the count across yields.

```rust
fn on_cpu_stop(&mut self, job: &mut Job, ran: Duration, why: Stop) {
    job.used_at_level += ran;                    // rule 4: yields do not clear this
    if job.used_at_level >= self.allotment[job.level] {
        job.level = (job.level + 1).min(self.levels - 1);
        job.used_at_level = Duration::ZERO;
    }
    match why {
        Stop::Blocked => self.waiting.push(job.id),
        _ => self.queues[job.level].push_back(job.id),
    }
}
```

This also shows why accounting must be exact. Old systems charged CPU time from the timer tick: the job on the CPU at the tick paid for the whole tick. A job that sleeps just before each tick then pays nothing. Tsafrir and others showed this "cheat" attack against real systems in 2007. Linux now charges each task from a nanosecond clock when it stops or at a tick (`update_curr()`).

## The boost period

<BoostSweepDemo />

1. Move $S$. Under the old rules, each boost gives Long one 10 ms quantum at the top. Its share is about 10 / $S$: 10% at $S$ = 100 ms.
2. Read the last two values. With no boost, Long gets 1.1% under the old rules and 48.9% under rule 4. Rule 4 removes this starvation, because the I/O jobs also sink.

$S$ has no correct value. If it is too large, long jobs starve and a job that changes its behavior waits a long time. If it is too small, interactive jobs lose their advantage. John Ousterhout called such numbers "voodoo constants": they need tuning, and nobody can derive them.

## Real systems

Most real schedulers in the 1980s to 2000s are variations on this idea. Quanta usually grow at lower levels, because a low level holds CPU-bound jobs that gain from fewer switches.

| System | Form of MLFQ |
|---|---|
| **BSD Unix** | It computes priority from recent CPU use, which decays over time. A formula takes the place of a queue move, and nice values add to it. |
| **Solaris** | Its time-sharing class has 60 levels. A table sets the quantum and the next level for each case. An administrator can change the table. |
| **Windows NT and later** | It has 32 priority levels. Threads in the dynamic range get a boost after I/O completes, and a thread that has starved gets a boost from a kernel helper. |
| **Linux $O(1)$, 2.6.0 to 2.6.22** | It has 140 levels, with active and expired arrays. Heuristics guessed which tasks were interactive. |
| **macOS (XNU)** | Timeshare threads lose priority as they use CPU and recover over time. Recent versions add the Clutch scheduler, which groups threads by application and QoS class. |

Linux replaced the $O(1)$ scheduler in 2.6.23, because its interactivity heuristics were fragile. The workloads that broke them were hard to predict and to debug. Its successor removes the guess: it does not classify tasks at all. It tracks how much CPU each task has had. The next lesson, [CFS and EEVDF](/os/scheduling/cfs-and-eevdf), explains this.
