A sampling profiler records thousands of stacks. A **flame graph** (Brendan Gregg, 2011) shows all of them in one picture:

- Each box is a function in a stack.
- The **width** of a box is the share of samples that have this stack. Wide boxes take much time.
- Callers are below and callees are above.
- The top edge of each box shows where the CPU really was (self time).

## Build one, sample by sample

<StacksToFlameDemo />

1. Click **Take 1 sample** a few times. Each sample is one stack, from `main` to the running function.
2. Each sample adds 1 to the width of every frame on its stack. Stacks with the same start share their lower boxes.
3. Click **Take 500**. The shape is now stable. The widths are the time shares.

## A method to read flame graphs

<Steps>
<div>Look at the top edge for wide boxes. The CPU spends its time there.</div>
<div>Follow a wide tower down to find the caller. Often the best fix is to call the function less.</div>
<div>Search for functions that appear in many towers, such as allocation, locks or logging. Their cost is spread out.</div>
<div>Do not read meaning into the colors of a classic flame graph. They are random warm colors.</div>
</Steps>

| Chart | x-axis | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| Flame graph | merged samples, sorted | where CPU time goes |
| Flame chart | time | what happened when, for one request |
| Off-CPU flame graph | merged waiting time | where threads wait |
| Differential flame graph | merged, colored by change | what got slower |

To make one: `perf record -g ./app`, then `perf script | stackcollapse-perf.pl | flamegraph.pl > out.svg`. You can also use `cargo flamegraph`, `samply record` or `go tool pprof -http=:8080`.
