"I made it 3% faster" is a statistical claim. Two runs of identical code differ by a few percent, so you need to know whether the difference you measured is bigger than that noise. Timing noise is also not normal: it is skewed right, with occasional large outliers. Both points change how you should compare.

1. Click **Run a new experiment** several times at 30 samples. The verdict flips between "faster" and "can't tell", from identical code and an identical true speedup.
2. Set the speedup to **0%**. Now any "faster" verdict is a false positive. It shows up only a few percent of the time (at most 2.5% for this one-sided check), which is what a 95% interval promises.
3. Set the speedup to **1%**. Even 1,000 samples per version only catch it some of the time. A 1% change is below what an unaided micro-benchmark can resolve in this noise.

## Doing it right

<Steps>
<div><b>Use robust statistics.</b> Compare medians (or trimmed means), not means: one context switch can move a mean by more than your optimization.</div>
<div><b>Quantify uncertainty with a bootstrap.</b> Resample each version's timings with replacement many times, compute the median ratio each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. This works for skewed distributions without assuming normality.</div>
<div><b>Decide on the sample size by effect size.</b> Halving the detectable effect needs about 4× the samples (the √n law from the CLT lesson).</div>
<div><b>Interleave A and B</b> (ABABAB…) rather than running all of A then all of B, so drift (thermals, background load) affects both equally.</div>
<div><b>Report the interval</b>, not just the point estimate: "B/A = 0.97 [0.95, 0.99]" says what you know; "3% faster" doesn't.</div>
</Steps>

Tools: Criterion.rs reports a bootstrap confidence interval and a change estimate against the previous run. `hyperfine` does the same for whole commands. Go's `benchstat` compares two sets of benchmark runs with a Mann-Whitney U test and reports `~` when the difference isn't significant. Statistics → Hypothesis testing covers the general theory.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Comparing many benchmarks">
With 40 benchmarks at a 5% false-positive rate each, you should expect about 2 "significant" changes from pure noise. Treat isolated small wins and regressions with suspicion, and correct for multiple comparisons when gating CI on performance.
</Callout>
