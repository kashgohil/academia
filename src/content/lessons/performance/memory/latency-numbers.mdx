Turn on **human scale** and read down the list: L1 is a second; DRAM is a minute and a half; a datacenter round trip is almost six days; a transatlantic round trip is two and a half years.

## The staircase on your machine

The numbers above are typical. The ones that matter are your machine's, and you can measure them with a pointer chase: a linked list laid out in random order inside a buffer of a given size. Each load depends on the previous one, so latencies can't overlap, and the time per step is the latency of whatever level the buffer fits in.

<LatencyStaircaseDemo />

The flat segments are the cache levels; the jumps are where the working set stops fitting. Real measurements (lmbench's `lat_mem_rd`, Intel MLC, or a 20-line pointer-chase loop) show the same shape, with softer edges, because of the TLB, replacement policy and shared L3.

## Rules of thumb that follow

<Steps>
<div><b>Count the slow operations, not the instructions.</b> A request that does 3 DRAM misses, 1 syscall and 1 network round trip is dominated entirely by the round trip.</div>
<div><b>Batch across expensive boundaries.</b> One round trip carrying 100 items beats 100 round trips: pipelining, batch APIs, vectored I/O.</div>
<div><b>Latency and bandwidth are different budgets.</b> DRAM latency is about 90 ns, but a core can stream about 10–20 GB/s, roughly 4 ns per 64-byte line, when accesses are predictable. Most of the next lessons are about turning latency-bound code into bandwidth-bound code.</div>
<div><b>Keep the numbers current.</b> Jeff Dean's original list (2009) put a disk seek at 10 ms and didn't mention SSDs. The ratios shift with each generation, so re-measure when it matters.</div>
</Steps>
