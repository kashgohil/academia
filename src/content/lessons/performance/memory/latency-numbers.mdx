Good performance decisions start with a few numbers. The numbers cover eight orders of magnitude. The large gaps between them are more important than the exact values. One extra DRAM access per item, or one extra network round trip per request, can cost more than all the computation.

Turn on **Human scale** and read the list from top to bottom:

1. An L1 cache hit takes 1 second.
2. A DRAM access takes 1.5 minutes.
3. A round trip in a datacenter takes almost 6 days.
4. A round trip from the US to Europe takes 2.5 years.

## Measure the levels on your machine

The values above are typical. You can measure the values for your machine with a **pointer chase**. Put a linked list in random order in a buffer. Each load needs the result of the previous load, so the loads cannot overlap. The time per step is the latency of the cache level that holds the buffer.

<LatencyStaircaseDemo />

The flat parts are the cache levels. The steps are where the buffer becomes too large for a level. Real measurements show the same shape. Use lmbench's `lat_mem_rd`, Intel MLC, or a short pointer-chase loop.

## Rules

<Steps>
<div>Count the slow operations, not the instructions. One network round trip costs more than thousands of instructions.</div>
<div>Batch work across slow boundaries. One round trip with 100 items is better than 100 round trips.</div>
<div>Latency and bandwidth are different limits. A DRAM access takes about 90 ns. But a core can stream about 10 to 20 GB/s when its accesses are predictable.</div>
<div>Measure again when it matters. The numbers change with each hardware generation.</div>
</Steps>
