Take a population, draw $n$ values, and calculate their average. Do this many times. The **central limit theorem** (CLT) says that the histogram of these averages approaches a normal curve as $n$ grows. This is true for almost all populations, whatever their shape. The CLT explains why the bell curve occurs so often, and why error bars decrease like $1/\sqrt{n}$. Its failures, when the variance is infinite, explain why some risks are much worse than averages show.

**Skewness** $\gamma$ measures how much longer one tail of a distribution is than the other. The **standardized mean** is the mean minus $\mu$, divided by $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Things to try:

1. **Exponential, n = 1, then 5, 30 and 300.** The histogram and the Q-Q plot become straighter. The skewness decreases slowly, as $1/\sqrt{n}$.
2. **Bimodal mixture, n = 2.** The mean of two draws has three peaks. Both draws can come from the left peak, both from the right peak, or one from each. At n ≈ 10, it looks almost normal.
3. **Bernoulli(0.1), n = 10.** The histogram has spikes, because the mean can only be 0, 0.1, 0.2, and so on. This is a **lattice** distribution. Its overall shape converges, but it never becomes smooth.
4. **Pareto(α = 1.5) and Cauchy.** Increase n to 500. The Pareto histogram improves slowly and keeps a long right tail. The Cauchy histogram does not improve at all.

## The statement

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent, with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. Then

$$
\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal N(0, 1).
$$

This statement has two separate parts:

- **Location and scale.** $\mathbb E[\bar X_n] = \mu$ and $\operatorname{SD}(\bar X_n) = \sigma/\sqrt n$. These are exact for every $n$. They need only a finite variance and independence. In the first demo, compare *SD of means (observed)* with *σ/√n (theory)*.
- **Shape.** The distribution of the standardized mean approaches the normal curve. This is the real theorem. It is only a statement about the limit.

<Callout title="The √n law in practice">
To halve the standard error, you need **four times** the data. To get one more decimal digit of precision, you need 100 times the data. This is the main reason why A/B tests are expensive and Monte Carlo methods converge slowly.
</Callout>

## Why it happens

The clearest proof uses **cumulants**. The first cumulant is the mean, and the second is the variance. The third and fourth cumulants measure skewness and tail weight. For independent variables, the cumulants of a sum are the sums of the cumulants.

<Steps>
<div>Let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$. For independent variables, each cumulant adds: $\kappa_r(S_n) = n\,\kappa_r(X)$.</div>
<div>Standardize: $Z_n = (S_n - n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$. A scale factor $c$ multiplies the $r$-th cumulant by $c^r$. So $\kappa_r(Z_n) = n\,\kappa_r(X)/(\sigma\sqrt n)^r \propto n^{1 - r/2}$.</div>
<div>For $r = 2$, the result is exactly 1, so the variance stays fixed. For $r = 3$, it decreases as $n^{-1/2}$, so the skewness goes away. For $r = 4$, it decreases as $n^{-1}$. Higher cumulants decrease even faster.</div>
<div>The normal distribution is the only distribution with all cumulants above order 2 equal to zero. So $Z_n$ approaches it. The sum removes every detail of the population shape, except its mean and variance.</div>
</Steps>

2. Select **Loaded (mostly 1)**. Its skewness is 1.47. At n = 30, the skewness is 0.27, which is 1.47/√30.
3. Look at the **largest CDF gap**. This is the largest difference between the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the sum and the normal CDF. For the fair die, it decreases from 0.143 with one die to 0.021 with 30 dice.
4. Select **Only 1 or 6**. The skewness is always 0, but the gap stays large: 0.072 at n = 30. The bars are 5 apart, so the CDF jumps in large steps.
5. Compare the gap with the **Berry–Esseen limit**. The gap is always below the limit.

The cumulants also give the **speed**. The largest error term is the skewness, of order $\gamma/\sqrt n$. The Berry–Esseen theorem gives a strict limit:

$$
\sup_x \left|P(Z_n \le x) - \Phi(x)\right| \le \frac{C\,\mathbb E|X-\mu|^3}{\sigma^3\sqrt n}, \qquad C < 0.48.
$$

Symmetric populations, such as the uniform and the bimodal mixture, have $\gamma = 0$. So they converge faster than skewed populations, such as the exponential or a Bernoulli with a small p. Rules such as "n ≥ 30" ignore this. Bernoulli(0.1) has skewness 2.67. For it, you need $np \gtrsim 10$, so n ≈ 100.

## Reading a Q-Q plot

Sort the standardized means. Plot each one against the normal quantile at the same rank. If the data are normal, the points are on the diagonal.

- **The right end curves up:** the right tail is heavier than normal (right skew).
- **An S shape:** both tails are heavier than normal.
- **Steps:** the data are discrete.

Q-Q plots show the behavior of the tails, which histograms hide. Use them to check the assumptions of a t-test or a regression.

## When the CLT fails

The theorem needs **finite variance** and **independence**. If one of them is missing, averages do not behave as expected.

**Pareto with α = 1.5** has a finite mean (μ = 3), but its variance is *infinite*. The sample mean still converges to μ, because the law of large numbers needs only a finite mean. But two things change:

- the fluctuations decrease like $n^{1/\alpha - 1} = n^{-1/3}$, not like $n^{-1/2}$;
- the limit shape is a skewed **α-stable** distribution, not a normal distribution.

This is the *generalized* CLT. File sizes, city populations, wealth, insurance losses and request latencies often have this shape. For such data, a summary such as "mean ± 2σ" has no meaning.

**Cauchy** has no mean at all. The average of $n$ standard Cauchy draws is *exactly* standard Cauchy for every $n$. Averages do not help, because one very large draw controls each sum.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Independence is as important as finite variance">
Samples with a positive correlation have a standard error much larger than σ/√n. Examples are users in the same session, adjacent time points, and servers in the same rack. With correlation ρ between all pairs, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\big(1 + (n-1)\rho\big)$, so it never goes to zero. With n = 100 and ρ = 0.1, the variance is 10.9 times larger, and the standard error is 3.3 times larger. Time series and clustered A/B tests must include this effect.
</Callout>

## Why this is important in other areas

- **Confidence intervals and t-tests** need a normal $\bar X$ (hypothesis testing module).
- **Measurement noise** is often almost normal, because it is the sum of many small independent effects.
- **Minibatch SGD noise** is almost normal for the same reason: it is an average of gradients over a batch. Its scale is proportional to $1/\sqrt{B}$. This scale affects the choice of the learning rate (Deep Learning → Optimization).
- **Benchmarks**: the mean of a heavy-tailed latency distribution converges slowly. Report percentiles instead (Performance → Measurement).
