Take any population, draw $n$ values, and average them. Repeat many times. The **central limit theorem** (CLT) says the histogram of those averages approaches a normal curve as $n$ grows, almost regardless of what the population looked like. It explains why the bell curve is everywhere, and why error bars shrink like $1/\sqrt{n}$. Its failures, when variance is infinite, explain why some risks are much worse than averages suggest.

Things to try:

1. **Exponential, n = 1 → 5 → 30 → 300.** Watch the histogram and the Q-Q plot straighten. The skew dies slowly, as $1/\sqrt{n}$.
2. **Bimodal mixture, n = 2.** The mean of two draws is trimodal (both draws from the same hump, or one from each). By n ≈ 10 it is hard to tell apart from normal.
3. **Bernoulli(0.1), n = 10.** The histogram is spiky because the mean can only take the values 0, 0.1, 0.2, … A *lattice* distribution converges in its overall shape, but never becomes smooth.
4. **Pareto(α = 1.5) and Cauchy.** Push n to 500. Pareto improves slowly and keeps a long right tail. Cauchy never improves at all.

## The statement

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. Then

$$
\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal N(0, 1).
$$

There are two separate claims here:

- **Location and scale.** $\mathbb E[\bar X_n] = \mu$ and $\operatorname{SD}(\bar X_n) = \sigma/\sqrt n$. These are exact for every $n$ and need only finite variance and independence. Compare *SD of means (observed)* with *σ/√n (theory)* in the demo.
- **Shape.** The standardized distribution tends to normal. This is the actual theorem, and it is only a statement about the limit.

<Callout title="The √n law, in practice">
To halve the standard error, you need **four times** the data. To gain one more decimal digit of precision, you need 100×. This is the main reason A/B tests are expensive and Monte Carlo methods converge slowly.
</Callout>

## Why it happens

The neatest argument uses *cumulants*: the mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis and so on, suitably scaled. Cumulants of independent sums add.

<Steps>
<div>Let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$. For independent variables, every cumulant adds: $\kappa_r(S_n) = n\,\kappa_r(X)$.</div>
<div>Standardize: $Z_n = (S_n - n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$. Scaling by $c$ multiplies the $r$-th cumulant by $c^r$, so $\kappa_r(Z_n) = n\,\kappa_r(X)/(\sigma\sqrt n)^r \propto n^{1 - r/2}$.</div>
<div>For $r = 2$ this is exactly 1, so the variance is fixed. For $r = 3$ it falls as $n^{-1/2}$, so skewness vanishes. For $r = 4$ it falls as $n^{-1}$, and higher cumulants vanish faster still.</div>
<div>The normal distribution is the unique distribution whose cumulants above order 2 are all zero, so $Z_n$ tends to it. Every shape detail of the population except its mean and variance is averaged away.</div>
</Steps>

This also tells you the **speed**: the leading error is skewness, of order $\gamma/\sqrt n$. The Berry–Esseen theorem makes it rigorous:

$$
\sup_x \left|P(Z_n \le x) - \Phi(x)\right| \le \frac{C\,\mathbb E|X-\mu|^3}{\sigma^3\sqrt n}, \qquad C < 0.48.
$$

Symmetric populations (uniform, bimodal) have $\gamma = 0$, so they converge visibly faster than skewed ones (exponential, Bernoulli with small p). Rules of thumb like "n ≥ 30" ignore this. For Bernoulli(0.1), you want $np \gtrsim 10$, which means n ≈ 100.

## Reading a Q-Q plot

Sort the standardized means and plot each one against the normal quantile at the same rank. If the data are normal, the points fall on the diagonal.

- **Curving up at the right end:** a heavier right tail than normal (right skew).
- **An S shape:** heavier tails on both sides.
- **Steps:** discreteness.

Q-Q plots show tail behavior that histograms hide. Use them when checking the assumptions behind a t-test or a regression.

## When the CLT fails

The theorem needs **finite variance** and **independence**. Break either one and averaging stops working as expected.

**Pareto with α = 1.5** has a finite mean (μ = 3) but *infinite* variance. The sample mean still converges to μ (the law of large numbers only needs a finite mean), but:

- the fluctuations shrink like $n^{1/\alpha - 1} = n^{-1/3}$ instead of $n^{-1/2}$;
- the limiting shape is a skewed **α-stable** distribution, not a normal.

This is the *generalized* CLT. File sizes, city populations, wealth, insurance losses and request latencies often look like this. A "mean ± 2σ" summary of such data is meaningless.

**Cauchy** has no mean at all. The average of $n$ standard Cauchy draws is *exactly* standard Cauchy for every $n$. Averaging does nothing, because one enormous draw dominates every sum.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Independence matters as much as variance">
Positively correlated samples (users in the same session, adjacent time points, servers in the same rack) have a standard error much larger than σ/√n. With correlation ρ between all pairs, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\big(1 + (n-1)\rho\big)$, so it never goes to zero. Time series and clustered A/B tests have to account for this.
</Callout>

## Why this matters elsewhere

- **Confidence intervals and t-tests** rest on the normality of $\bar X$ (Hypothesis testing module).
- **Measurement noise** is often roughly normal because it is the sum of many small independent effects.
- **Minibatch SGD noise** is roughly normal for the same reason: a gradient averaged over a batch. Its scale is $\propto 1/\sqrt{B}$, which matters for learning-rate choice (Deep Learning → Optimization).
- **Benchmarking**: the mean of a heavy-tailed latency distribution converges slowly. Report percentiles instead (Performance → Measurement).
