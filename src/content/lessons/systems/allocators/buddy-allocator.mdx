The buddy system restricts every block to a power-of-two size, aligned to its own size. That restriction pays off. Splitting a block yields two halves, called **buddies**. A block's buddy can be computed rather than searched for, so merging on free is a handful of operations per level. Linux manages physical pages this way: orders 0 to 10, from 4 KiB to 4 MiB, visible in `/proc/buddyinfo`.

Things to try:

1. **Four 100 B, free in order**. Each 100 B request takes a 128 B block. Watch the free-block list as they are freed: buddies merge only when *both* halves are free, so the full 1 KiB block reappears only at the last free.
2. Allocate 129 B: you get a 256 B block, and almost half of it is slack. Power-of-two rounding has a worst case just under 50% internal waste.
3. Allocate one 16 B block, then try 513 B. It fails: the 1 KiB block is split, and the small allocation **pins** its 512 B half.
4. Choose **align 64** with a small request: the block size becomes 64, because blocks are naturally aligned to their size.

## Why XOR finds the buddy

<Steps>
<div>A block of size $2^k$ always starts at a multiple of $2^k$, so bit $k$ of its offset says whether it is the lower or upper half of its parent.</div>
<div>The buddy differs from it in exactly that bit: $\text{buddy}(o, 2^k) = o \oplus 2^k$.</div>
<div>The parent starts at $\min(o, \text{buddy})$, i.e. $o$ with bit $k$ cleared.</div>
<div>Free: while the buddy at this order is free, remove it from its free list and move up one order. That's at most $\log_2(\text{heap}/\text{min})$ steps.</div>
</Steps>

Allocation is the mirror image: take the smallest free order that fits, then split down, pushing each upper half onto its order's free list. Each order's free list can be a simple list or a bitmap; Linux uses lists per order plus migrate types.

## Trade-offs

| | Buddy |
|---|---|
| Internal fragmentation | up to ~50% (about 25% on average for uniform sizes) |
| External fragmentation | limited: free blocks merge eagerly, but one live block pins every ancestor |
| Metadata | an order per block, kept out of band (or a bit per block pair) |
| Speed | O(log n) worst case, usually O(1) |

That is why the buddy allocator usually sits at the **bottom** of a stack. It hands out pages or large runs cheaply, and a slab or size-class allocator on top carves those into small objects without power-of-two waste.
