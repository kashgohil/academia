A **buddy allocator** gives out blocks whose sizes are powers of two. Each block starts at a multiple of its own size. When the allocator splits a block, it gets two equal halves. These two halves are **buddies**. The allocator can calculate the address of a buddy, so it does not search for it. Thus a free can merge blocks again with a few operations per level.

The **order** of a block tells its size. A block of order $k$ is $2^k$ times the minimum block. Linux uses a buddy allocator for physical memory pages. It has orders 0 to 10, from 4 KiB (one page) to 4 MiB. The file `/proc/buddyinfo` shows the number of free blocks in each order.

## Why XOR finds the buddy

<Steps>
<div>A block of size $2^k$ always starts at a multiple of $2^k$. So bit $k$ of its offset tells if the block is the lower half or the upper half of its parent.</div>
<div>The buddy is different from the block only in that bit: $\text{buddy}(o, 2^k) = o \oplus 2^k$.</div>
<div>The parent starts at the lower of the two offsets. That is $o$ with bit $k$ set to 0.</div>
<div>To free a block, the allocator does this: while the buddy is free and has the same order, remove the buddy from its free list and go up one order.</div>
<div>For a heap of $N$ bytes and a minimum block of $m$ bytes, a free takes at most $\log_2(N/m)$ steps. In the demo, that is 6 steps.</div>
</Steps>

Allocation is the opposite process. The allocator finds the smallest free order that fits. Then it splits down, and it puts each upper half on the free list of its order. Each order can keep its free blocks in a list or in a bitmap. Linux keeps one list per order and per migration type. A **migration type** groups pages by whether the kernel can move them.

## Trade-offs

Waste inside a block is **internal fragmentation**. A request of $2^k + 1$ bytes gets a block of $2^{k+1}$ bytes. So the worst case is just under 50% (49.95% for a request of 1025 B). For request sizes from 129 B to 2 KiB, chosen uniformly, the average waste is 25% of the block.

| | Buddy |
|---|---|
| Internal fragmentation | just under 50% in the worst case, 25% on average for uniform sizes |
| External fragmentation | limited: free buddies merge at once, but one live block keeps all its ancestors split |
| Metadata | the order of each block, kept outside the block (or one bit for each pair of buddies) |
| Speed | at most $O(\log n)$ steps, usually $O(1)$ |

For this reason, a buddy allocator usually sits at the bottom of a stack of allocators. It gives out pages and large runs at low cost. A slab allocator or a size-class allocator on top cuts these pages into small objects without power-of-two waste.
