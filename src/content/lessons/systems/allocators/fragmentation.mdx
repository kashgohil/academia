Fragmentation means memory that is reserved but cannot be used for the program's data. It has two forms you have now seen in every allocator:

- **Internal**: waste *inside* allocated blocks, from headers, alignment padding and rounding up to a class or power of two.
- **External**: free memory split into pieces too small for the requests that arrive.

A single number captures both. Replay the same allocation trace through each allocator, and record the highest address it ever needed, its **footprint**. Divide by the peak amount of live data at any moment. A ratio of 1.0 is perfect packing. This is the measure Wilson, Johnstone and colleagues used in their 1990s studies of real allocators.

Things to try:

1. On **Random sizes and lifetimes**, tick *next fit*. Its footprint climbs almost immediately: next fit keeps advancing through fresh memory instead of reusing holes behind it.
2. Compare **first fit** with and without **coalescing**: about 1.16× vs 2.23×. Coalescing is not optional.
3. Switch to **Request-scoped (server)**. Every request frees everything it allocated. A per-request arena would match this lifetime pattern exactly, with a footprint close to live data and no per-object frees.
4. On **Growing buffers**, the policies converge. The big doubling buffers dominate, and they fit power-of-two blocks exactly, so the buddy allocator does as well as best fit.

## What actually controls fragmentation

<Steps>
<div><b>Placement policy.</b> Policies that reuse memory close to recent allocations and keep large blocks intact (best fit, address-ordered first fit) fragment least. On real program traces, Johnstone and Wilson (1998) measured both at only a few percent.</div>
<div><b>Coalescing.</b> Merging neighbors immediately (or before memory is needed) turns many small holes back into large ones.</div>
<div><b>Rounding.</b> Size classes and buddy blocks buy speed with internal waste; the class layout controls the trade-off.</div>
<div><b>Lifetimes, most of all.</b> Fragmentation comes from <i>interleaving</i> objects that die at different times. Long-lived small objects scattered among short-lived large ones pin holes open (the <b>Small pins between big frees</b> workload).</div>
</Steps>

<Callout title="Synthetic traces exaggerate">
Uniformly random sizes and lifetimes are the worst kind of test workload. Real programs allocate in phases, reuse a few sizes and free objects in clusters, and allocators exploit exactly that. Wilson et al. argued that random-trace studies made fragmentation look far worse than it is in practice. Use these demos to understand mechanisms, and measure your real program before choosing an allocator.
</Callout>

## Tools that don't depend on the allocator

In C, C++, Rust, Zig and Odin, pointers are raw addresses, so an allocator can never **move** a live object to close a gap. (Compacting garbage collectors can, which is one of their real advantages.) That leaves you with structural fixes:

- **Group by lifetime**: arenas per frame, request or phase. This removes fragmentation instead of managing it.
- **Group by size or type**: pools and slabs for hot, uniform objects.
- **Avoid pinning**: allocate long-lived objects up front or in their own region, away from churn.
- **Reserve capacity**: grow containers geometrically, or reserve once, to avoid the free-old/allocate-new churn of growing buffers.
- **Return memory to the OS**: allocators keep freed pages for reuse. jemalloc's decay settings, mimalloc's purge delays and glibc's `malloc_trim` control when memory goes back.
