**Fragmentation** is memory that the allocator holds but that the program cannot use for its data. There are two kinds, and each allocator in this module has one or both:

- **Internal fragmentation** is waste inside allocated blocks. It comes from headers, alignment padding and rounding up to a size class or a power of two.
- **External fragmentation** is free memory that is split into pieces. Each piece is too small for the requests that arrive.

## See both kinds

The demo measures external fragmentation as the part of free memory that is outside the largest free block:

$$\text{external} = 1 - \frac{\text{largest free block}}{\text{total free memory}}$$

When this value is high, a large request can fail although much memory is free.

## One number for a whole workload

A **trace** is a recorded list of the allocations and frees of a program. Replay the same trace through each allocator. Record the highest address that the allocator ever used. This is its **footprint**. Divide the footprint by the peak amount of live data. A ratio of 1.0 is perfect packing.

This ratio includes both kinds of fragmentation. Wilson, Johnstone and others used this measure in their studies of real allocators in the 1990s.

## What controls fragmentation

<Steps>
<div><b>Placement policy.</b> Some policies reuse memory near recent allocations and keep large blocks whole. Best fit and address-ordered first fit do this, and they fragment least. On real program traces, Johnstone and Wilson (1998) measured only a few percent for both.</div>
<div><b>Coalescing.</b> When the allocator merges free neighbors at once (or before it needs the memory), many small holes become large holes again.</div>
<div><b>Rounding.</b> Size classes and buddy blocks make allocation faster, but they add internal waste. The layout of the classes controls this trade-off.</div>
<div><b>Lifetimes, most of all.</b> Fragmentation comes from objects that are next to each other but die at different times. Small long-lived objects between large short-lived ones keep the holes apart. The <b>Small pins between big frees</b> workload shows this.</div>
</Steps>

<Callout title="Synthetic traces make fragmentation look worse">
Random sizes and random lifetimes are the worst kind of test workload. Real programs allocate in phases, use a few sizes again and again, and free objects in groups. Allocators use these patterns. Wilson and his colleagues showed that studies with random traces made fragmentation look much worse than it is in practice. Use these demos to learn the mechanisms. Measure your real program before you choose an allocator.
</Callout>

## Fixes that do not depend on the allocator

In C, C++, Rust, Zig and Odin, pointers are raw addresses. So an allocator can never **move** a live object to close a gap. A compacting garbage collector can move objects, and this is one of its real advantages. Without one, you must change the structure of the program:

- **Group by lifetime.** Use arenas per frame, per request or per phase. This removes fragmentation, instead of managing it.
- **Group by size or type.** Use pools and slabs for hot objects of one size.
- **Prevent pinning.** Allocate long-lived objects at the start or in their own region, away from short-lived ones.
- **Reserve capacity.** Grow containers geometrically, or reserve their size once. This prevents the cycle of free-old, allocate-new that growing buffers cause.
- **Give memory back to the OS.** Allocators keep freed pages for reuse. The decay settings of jemalloc, the purge delays of mimalloc and `malloc_trim` in glibc control when memory goes back.
