To support arbitrary sizes *and* arbitrary frees, the allocator has to remember each block's size and find free space. The classic design, used in K&R `malloc` and in the textbooks, is the **free list**. Every block starts with a small header recording its size and whether it is in use. Allocating means finding a free block that is big enough, **splitting** off the remainder, and returning the bytes after the header. Freeing marks the block free and, ideally, **coalesces** it with free neighbors.

Things to try:

1. Run **Fill, free all, ask for 1 KiB** with coalescing off, then on.
2. Run **Swiss cheese**. Alternate 100 B and 40 B blocks, then free the 100 B ones. Coalescing can't help here, because live 40 B blocks separate the holes. External fragmentation goes above 50%, and a 200 B request fails despite hundreds of free bytes.
3. Switch **Placement** and **Churn** for a while. Watch where new blocks land: first fit packs the low addresses, next fit sweeps across the whole heap, and worst fit keeps carving up the biggest block.
4. Allocate 1 B. It costs 16 B: an 8 B header plus the minimum payload. Small objects are expensive on a general free list.

## Block anatomy

<Steps>
<div><b>Header</b>: the block size, a multiple of 8, so the low 3 bits are always zero. One of them stores the "allocated" flag for free.</div>
<div><b>Splitting</b>: if the chosen block exceeds the request by at least the minimum block size, carve off the remainder as a new free block. Otherwise hand over the whole block; the leftover becomes slack.</div>
<div><b>Coalescing forward</b>: the next block's header is at `block + size`, so that's free to check.</div>
<div><b>Coalescing backward</b> needs the *previous* block's size. Knuth's <b>boundary tags</b> copy the size into a footer at the end of each free block, so the previous block's footer is just before your header.</div>
</Steps>

## Placement policies

| Policy | Chooses | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| First fit | lowest-addressed block that fits | good; small fragments collect near the start |
| Next fit | first fit, starting where the last search ended | spreads allocations everywhere: worse fragmentation and locality |
| Best fit | smallest block that fits | excellent memory use; needs a size-ordered structure to be fast |
| Worst fit | largest block | destroys big blocks; poor |

Fragmentation, the last lesson of this module, measures these on the same workloads.

## Making it fast

A single list scanned linearly is O(number of blocks). Real allocators use:

- **Explicit free lists**: link only the free blocks, threading the pointers through their payloads.
- **Segregated free lists**: one list per size range, which approximates best fit at near O(1). dlmalloc uses small "bins" for exact sizes and trees for large ones.
- **Deferred coalescing**: keep recently freed blocks unmerged for quick reuse (glibc's tcache and fastbins), and coalesce later.
